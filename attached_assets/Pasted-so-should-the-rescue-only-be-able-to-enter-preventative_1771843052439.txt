Yes, absolutely. Keeping them strictly separated is the best practice for veterinary and rescue software. The UI in your screenshot looks exceptionally clean and handles this perfectly.
With your extensive background in clinical medication management, the reasoning behind this architectural split will make perfect sense. Just as a clinical environment separates acute therapeutic drugs from routine prophylactics, your software needs to separate them to keep the data clean and actionable.
Here is why you should strictly enforce this rule for your users:
1. The "Active Illness" vs. "Routine Care" Divide
The Meds Tab (Active Treatment): This should be reserved exclusively for drugs tied to a specific diagnosis, symptom, or behavioral modification plan. These are medications like a 10-day course of Doxycycline for kennel cough, or daily Gabapentin for post-op pain. They require specific dosing instructions (e.g., mg/kg), frequencies (BID/TID), and usually have a defined start and stop date.
The Preventative Tab (Routine Maintenance): This is for population-wide protocols that are administered regardless of the animal's current health status, acting as standard intake and maintenance care. These rely entirely on the "Event + Trigger" due-date logic we just built, rather than a daily treatment sheet.
2. Protecting the Treatment Dashboard
If you allow volunteers to log Simparica or vaccines in the "Meds" tab, it completely clutters the active medical roster.
A shelter manager or vet tech looking at a "Current Medications" report needs to instantly see which animals are actively sick or require immediate daily intervention. If that critical report is flooded with hundreds of dogs simply getting their routine monthly flea pill, the truly sick dogs get lost in the noise, which becomes a patient safety issue.
3. Inventory and Compliance Tracking
Software designed for shelters handles inventory differently for these two categories. Preventatives are often ordered and tracked in massive bulk quantities and administered to the whole population, whereas acute medications (especially controlled substances) require incredibly strict, patient-specific logging and error-catching. Separating them at the UI level ensures your backend database routes the inventory deductions correctly.
How to Enforce This in iRescue.life
Because volunteers are moving fast, someone will eventually try to type "Simparica" into the Meds tab. You need a guardrail.
This type of inline redirection is a recognized UX best practice because it guides the user at the point of action without breaking their workflow. Providing hints directly inside the dropdown reduces form errors and ensures your data stays perfectly segregated.

I need to add a "Smart Interceptor" to the Medications Dropdown on the Active Meds tab. This will prevent users from logging routine preventatives in the wrong place and train them to use the correct UI.
1. The Logic (Frontend/State):
When the user types into the MedicationSearch input on the Meds tab, capture the search string.
Run a cross-check against the PreventativeProtocols table (or a cached array of preventative names like Simparica, NexGard, ProHeart).
2. The UI (The Dropdown Rendering):
If the search string matches an active therapeutic medication, display it normally.
If the search string matches a known preventative:
Inject a special, disabled list item at the top of the dropdown results.
Styling: Give it a distinct background (like a soft yellow warning) and an info icon.
Text: "Looking for [Search Term]? Please log routine preventatives under the 'Preventative' tab to accurately track due dates."
Ensure this item is visually distinct and unclickable so it cannot be submitted.
3. Fallback (Backend Validation):
On the backend submit function for the "Active Meds" form, add a strict validation check. If the submitted medication_name or id belongs to the Preventatives category, reject the submission and return a clear error: "Preventatives must be logged in the Preventative tab."
